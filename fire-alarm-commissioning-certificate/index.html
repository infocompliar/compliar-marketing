Fire alarm commissioning certificate: what it covers and who signs it

BS 5839-1 provides for a certificate at each stage of a new fire detection and alarm system: design, installation, commissioning and acceptance. This guide is about the commissioning one: what it records, how it fits with the others, what to check before you sign it and the mistakes that cause trouble at handover or at a scheme audit.

General guidance only. Check the standard itself and take advice from your own competent person for the job in front of you. Clause and annex references are to BS 5839-1:2025; earlier editions number things differently, so check the edition you are working to. Last reviewed 3 November 2026.

Four certificates, four responsibilities

A new non-domestic fire detection and alarm system is rarely designed, installed and commissioned by one person, and BS 5839-1 keeps the responsibilities apart by providing a model certificate for each stage in Annex G. Each one is signed by the organisation that did that part of the work:

CertificateWho signsWhat it confirms
DesignThe designerThe system category, the extent of coverage, the design decisions and any agreed variations from the standard.
InstallationThe installerThat the wiring and equipment were installed to the design and to the standard, with the installer's tests done.
CommissioningThe commissioning engineerThat the complete system was tested and works as designed, with any variations and outstanding items recorded.
AcceptanceThe user or purchaserThat the system, the documentation and the training have been handed over and accepted.

Where one company does everything, it signs all four. Where the installer and commissioning engineer are different companies, each signs its own, and the acceptance certificate is where the customer confirms they received the lot. A separate verification certificate exists for the case where a third party is asked to check an installed system; in Compliar it is a separate template.

What the commissioning certificate records

Commissioning is the point at which someone takes responsibility for saying the system works. BS 5839-1 Section 5 sets out what has to be done, and the model certificate in Annex G reflects it. In practice the certificate records:

  • The site and the system. Site details, the designed category (L1 to L5, M, P1 or P2), the panel, the number of zones or loops, and the extent of the system being commissioned, including any part that is not yet complete.
  • Every device tested. Each detector, manual call point, sounder, visual alarm device and interface operated and shown to work, and the addresses or zone text checked against the zone plan.
  • Cause and effect. That the programmed cause and effect matches the design: which devices trigger which outputs, delays, staged alarms, plant shutdowns, door releases and lift grounding.
  • Sound and visual alarm levels. Sound pressure levels at the required points, and visual alarm coverage where it is relied on.
  • Standby power. The standby battery capacity against the system's quiescent and alarm loads for the required standby and alarm durations. The battery calculator shows the arithmetic.
  • Alarm transmission. That signals reach the alarm receiving centre, where the system is monitored, and that fault signals are received too.
  • Documentation. That the as-fitted drawings, zone plan, operating instructions, log book and the installer's test results are in place.
  • Variations and outstanding work. Any agreed departure from the standard, and anything not finished at the date of commissioning, so that nobody later assumes it was.
  • False alarm precautions. That the measures to limit false alarms were considered and that the user has been told how to manage them.

The exact wording of the model certificate, and the list of items under each heading, is in the standard. Use it as the reference, not this page.

Before you sign

What to check on commissioning day

Addressable or conventional

On an addressable system, walk the loop and confirm every address reads at the panel with the right location text. On a conventional system, confirm each zone's devices operate and that the zone text on the panel matches the zone plan.

Zone plan at the panel

The zone plan is part of the system, not an extra. Check it is fixed next to the panel, that it is orientated the way the reader faces, and that it matches the as-fitted drawing. Our zone plan guide covers what it has to show.

Batteries, not just the label

Measure the quiescent and alarm currents rather than copying them from the design. If the as-installed loads are higher than the design assumed, the fitted batteries may be too small.

Interfaces one by one

Door holders, access control releases, lift recall, plant shutdown, gas valves: operate each one from a real alarm, not from the panel's test menu, and record the result for each.

Sound levels where it matters

Take readings in the places people sleep or work with the doors shut, not only in corridors. Record the level and the location.

Tell the ARC

Put the system on test with the alarm receiving centre before you start, confirm fire and fault signals both arrive, and take it off test when you finish. Record the time of each.

Common mistakes

  • Commissioning before the installation is finished. If there is outstanding work, the certificate must say so. A clean certificate on an unfinished system is the thing an auditor, or a fire investigator, will find first.
  • Copying the design loads. Measured loads are what the standby battery calculation needs.
  • One tick for cause and effect. Record the matrix item by item. "Cause and effect checked" tells the next engineer nothing about which outputs were operated.
  • Variations that are not written down. A department store that agreed to keep manual call points off the shop floor has a variation. If it is not on the design and commissioning certificates, the next servicing engineer will raise it as a defect.
  • No handover of the log book. The log book is where the user records the weekly test and every event afterwards. Hand it over, show them how to use it, and record that you did on the acceptance certificate.
  • Mixing up commissioning and verification. Verification is an independent check of a system someone else installed. If you are asked to verify, use the verification certificate.

FAQ

Common questions

Who signs a fire alarm commissioning certificate?

The organisation that commissioned the system, through a competent commissioning engineer. If the same company designed and installed the system it also signs the design and installation certificates; the customer signs the acceptance certificate.

Is the commissioning certificate the same as the installation certificate?

No. The installation certificate confirms the wiring and equipment were installed to the design and the standard. The commissioning certificate confirms the complete system was tested and works as designed.

Does a commissioning certificate mean the system is compliant?

It records that the system was commissioned and tested, and lists any variations and outstanding work. Whether the design is right for the building is a design responsibility, and the certificate does not replace the fire risk assessment.

What if there is outstanding work on commissioning day?

Record it on the certificate. The standard provides for outstanding items to be listed so that nobody later assumes the system was complete. Commission the completed part and say what is still to do.

Do I need a verification certificate as well?

Only when a third party is asked to check a system installed by someone else. Verification is a separate certificate and a separate exercise from commissioning.

Which edition of BS 5839-1 does Compliar reference?

The templates reference BS 5839-1:2025. Clause numbers on this page are for that edition; check the edition your contract names.

Can the certificate be filled in with no signal?

Yes. The field app runs in the phone's browser and saves the certificate on the phone, sending it when back in range. Opening the PDF of a certificate issued offline needs a connection.
