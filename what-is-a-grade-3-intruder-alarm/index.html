What is a Grade 3 intruder alarm?

Grade 3 is the grade most commercial premises are specified to, and the one insurers often ask for. Here is what it means and what it involves.

General guidance only. Check the standard itself and take advice from your own competent person for the job in front of you. Last reviewed 2 November 2026.

The short answer

A Grade 3 intruder alarm is a system designed to resist an intruder with good knowledge of the system and a comprehensive set of tools, including portable electronics. The grade comes from the BS EN 50131 series, and PD 6662 explains how those European standards are applied in the UK.

How Grade 3 compares

GradeIntruder assumed
1Little knowledge of the system and a limited range of tools.
2Some knowledge, using general tools and some specialist equipment.
3Good knowledge of the system and a comprehensive set of tools, including portable electronics.
4Able to plan in detail, with the full range of equipment and the means to substitute vital components.

Where Grade 3 is used

Grade 3 is typical for larger homes and most commercial premises, and insurers often ask for it where an alarm is a condition of cover. The grade should be chosen from a risk assessment and written into the specification. Check the policy wording before you design, because insurers set their own requirements.

Every part has to be Grade 3

A system takes the grade of its lowest-graded component. A Grade 3 control panel with Grade 2 detectors is a Grade 2 system. When you quote or inspect a Grade 3 system, check the panel, detectors, keypads, sounders, communicator and power supply, and keep the product grades on file.

Grade 3 and police response

The grade alone does not get a police response. Under the National Police Chiefs' Council requirements, the system must be monitored by a recognised alarm receiving centre, installed and maintained by a company certified by a UKAS-accredited body, and the premises must hold a unique reference number (URN) from the police force. Police policy is reviewed from time to time, so use the current document for anything you put in a contract.

Maintenance and standby power

  • Visits. NSI's member guidance table sets two visits a year for grades 2, 3 and 4, and for grades 2 and 3 one of those can be a remote check if the equipment supports it. Check your own certification body, the police requirements and the insurer.
  • Standby power. Power supply manufacturers publish 30 hours of standby for Grade 3 where a mains failure is reported to the alarm receiving centre, and 60 hours where it is not. Use the figure in the manufacturer's data sheet and your specification.

Grade 3 compared with Grade 2 and Grade 4

BS EN 50131 sets four security grades. Grade 1 is for low risk, where the intruder is expected to have little knowledge of alarm systems. Grade 2 is for low to medium risk. Grade 3 is for medium to high risk, where the intruder is expected to be knowledgeable and to carry a comprehensive range of tools and portable electronic equipment. Grade 4 is for high risk, such as sites where security takes priority over cost. Each step up asks more of the equipment in areas like tamper protection, signalling and power supply.

What changes in practice at Grade 3

  • Equipment. Detectors, control panel, keypads, sounders and the power supply are tested to the grade and carry a certificate or declaration for that grade.
  • Tamper and fault monitoring. The system is expected to detect and report attempts to open or interfere with its parts, and faults such as low battery or loss of a communication path.
  • Access control of the system. User codes, set and unset methods and engineer access are arranged so that a lower-level user cannot reach engineering functions.
  • Alarm transmission. Where the alarm is passed to a monitoring centre, the signalling path is specified and graded as well, with its own checks and monitoring.

What an installer records

A Grade 3 job should leave a clear record: the system grade, the equipment fitted with model references, the zones and what each covers, how the system is set and unset, the signalling arrangement, and the test results. Those same details are what the next engineer needs at the first maintenance visit.

Choosing a grade

The grade follows from a risk assessment of the premises and the contents, and is often set by the insurer or the certification scheme the installer works under. If the insurer states a grade, get it in writing and put it on the paperwork.

FAQ

Common questions

What is a Grade 3 intruder alarm?

A Grade 3 intruder alarm is designed to resist an intruder with good knowledge of the system and a comprehensive set of tools, including portable electronics. The grade comes from the BS EN 50131 series.

Is Grade 3 better than Grade 2?

Grade 3 assumes a more capable intruder than Grade 2, so its equipment is built and tested to resist more. Which one a building needs depends on a risk assessment and the insurer.

Do I need Grade 3 for insurance?

Insurers often ask for Grade 3 where an alarm is a condition of cover, but each insurer sets its own requirement. Check the policy wording.

Does a Grade 3 alarm get a police response?

Not on its own. The system must be monitored by a recognised alarm receiving centre, installed and maintained by a company certified by a UKAS-accredited body, and the police force must issue a unique reference number for the premises.

How often does a Grade 3 intruder alarm need servicing?

NSI's member guidance table sets two visits a year for grades 2, 3 and 4, with one of them allowed to be a remote check for grades 2 and 3 where the equipment supports it. Check the requirements of your certification body, the police force and the insurer.

What is the difference between Grade 2 and Grade 3?

Grade 2 is for low to medium risk and assumes an intruder with limited knowledge. Grade 3 is for medium to high risk and assumes a knowledgeable intruder with a range of tools. Equipment and installation requirements are stricter at Grade 3.

Can I mix Grade 2 and Grade 3 equipment?

The system is only as good as its lowest-graded component, so a Grade 3 specification needs Grade 3 parts throughout, including the power supply and signalling.

Who tells me which grade I need?

Usually the risk assessment, the insurer, or the standards of the certification body the installer is registered with. Ask for the requirement in writing and record it on the design and commissioning documents.
